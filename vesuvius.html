The Vesuvius Challenge · Independent research · Ongoing

Reading a scroll
no one can open.

Hundreds of ancient scrolls survived Mount Vesuvius as sealed blocks of carbon. The Vesuvius Challenge asks whether X-rays and machine learning can recover the writing inside — without physically opening them. I am building a research system to help find out.

The challenge

A library survived.
The pages did not.

In 79 CE, Mount Vesuvius buried the Roman town of Herculaneum. In one villa sat a private library — hundreds of papyrus scrolls. The heat did not burn them to ash. It carbonized them, turning each scroll into a fragile block of solid carbon.

They are the only library to survive intact from the ancient world. But they cannot be read. Try to unroll one and it crumbles. The writing is sealed inside, wound in hundreds of crushed layers.

So the scrolls are not opened at all. They are taken to a particle accelerator and scanned with X-rays, the same idea as a medical CT scan, at a resolution fine enough to see a single sheet of papyrus. The question is what you do with the scan once you have it.

How you read a closed scroll

Four moves, on one
untouched object.

01Scan
  1. Scan

    X-rays turn the scroll into thousands of detailed slices.

    A synchrotron captures the sealed scroll as a stack of cross-sections — a full three-dimensional volume, without ever touching it.

  2. Separate

    Software traces one crushed sheet through the volume.

    Inside the block, the layers are pressed together and warped. Software follows a single sheet of papyrus through the scan, wrap after wrap.

  3. Flatten

    That warped sheet becomes a flat image.

    The traced surface is unrolled and flattened into a readable plane — the digital equivalent of laying the page out on a table.

  4. Read

    A model searches the surface for physical traces left by ink.

    Ancient ink is carbon on carbon, nearly invisible. A model looks across the flattened surface for the faint physical signature the ink left behind.

What fooled us

The first models found
“ink” everywhere.

Early on, I ran released ink-detection models across surfaces from an unopened scroll. Different models lit up the same places. When independent models agree, it feels like confirmation.

It was not. Looking closer, the bright marks were papyrus fibers, seams, torn edges, and surface texture — the natural grain of a two-thousand-year-old page. The models were agreeing on the paper, not on letters.

The lesson

Agreement is not proof.

Building proof

So I built a system
designed to prove
itself wrong.

I stopped asking whether a mark looked like a letter. I started asking what would prove that it is not one. Every claim has to map back to the physical scroll and survive tests built to break it.

  1. Published writing

    Start from text an expert has already read and published.

  2. Exact surface pixels

    Tie that writing to the precise pixels on the flattened surface.

  3. Original CT volume

    Trace those pixels back down into the original X-ray scan.

  4. Adversarial tests

    Attack the result with chance baselines and false-positive checks.

  5. Sealed result

    Only what survives is written down. A failed test ends the path.

The rule

Every mark must return to a real place in the scan.

If a candidate letter cannot be traced from the published image, to an exact surface pixel, to a voxel in the original CT volume, it is not evidence. It is a picture.

Fail closed

A failed test ends the path — it never becomes a prettier claim.

One gate

I make the claims. The system is built so nothing approves itself.

Cohors Vesuviana

A research team
built to disagree.

To keep the work moving, I built a small autonomous research cohort — six specialized roles that investigate, build, challenge, and monitor experiments. They are deliberately separated so that no single agent can both make a claim and approve it. I remain the orchestrator and the only approval gate.

  • Chooses the one question worth answering next, and the cheapest test that could kill it.

  • Builds the experimental machinery — pipelines, storage, and the tests that run against them.

  • Hunts across scroll surfaces for defensible, letter-scale signal worth escalating.

  • Attempts to break every claim before it is believed — the adversary inside the team.

  • Monitors new data, prior art, and the wider community for anything that changes the odds.

  • Holds the limits — compute, permissions, and spend — so the work stays inside its guardrails.

What we know now

No letters yet.
A much more
truthful instrument.

  • Proven

    The scan-to-score pipeline works on writing we already know. It runs end to end, from a published image to a score, without breaking.

  • Traced

    51 published characters from scroll PHerc0139 were traced back to their exact physical locations inside the original CT volume.

  • Discovered

    On PHerc1667, the useful signal was spatial coverage — where the model predicts ink — not how strongly it predicts. That distinction survived every frozen test.

  • Honest boundary

    With that rule frozen, the six locally available PHerc1447 surfaces produced no defensible letter-scale candidates above chance. Not a discovery. A clean edge around what the current model can do.

Artwork in production

The work continues. The next direction is intentionally not frozen yet — the point of the instrument is to stay honest about what it cannot yet see.
